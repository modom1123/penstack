How to Audit an Inherited Shopify Catalog

A practical order of operations for untangling product structure, content, images, SEO, collections, and merchandising.

pBy the penstack team
September 30, 2026 · 10 min read

Inherited catalogs rarely fail in one dramatic way. They usually fail through hundreds of small decisions nobody remembers making.

You open the store and find duplicate products, inconsistent product types, thin descriptions, old images, tags that mean different things, collections that overlap, and SEO fields that were filled in by whoever happened to be working that day.

The instinct is often to start rewriting product pages immediately. That is usually the wrong first move. If the structure underneath the content is still messy, you will rewrite the same problems into cleaner sentences.

The short answer

Audit in this order: structure → content → images → SEO → collections → merchandising. Fix the foundation first so later improvements do not create more rework.

The Shopify catalog audit framework

Work through the catalog in an order that reduces dependencies. Structure comes first because content, SEO, navigation, and merchandising all rely on it.

1. Start with product structure before rewriting copy.

Product structure is the part of the catalog most likely to create downstream chaos. If product types, variants, tags, vendors, and structured fields are inconsistent, every other audit becomes harder.

Start by identifying the system the store is actually using, not the system somebody intended it to use.

CheckWhat to look forTypical fix
Product typesDuplicates, near-duplicates, vague namesNormalize to a documented set
VariantsInconsistent option names and valuesStandardize Size, Color, Material, etc.
Structured fieldsImportant details buried only in descriptionsMove reusable data into metafields
VendorDifferent spellings or display namesChoose one canonical vendor value
TagsDuplicate, temporary, or undocumented labelsRetire, normalize, or document

If the catalog does not have a clear classification system yet, start with How to Build a Product Taxonomy Before Your Catalog Becomes a Mess.

2. Audit product content for gaps, duplication, and accuracy.

Once the structure is stable enough to trust, review the customer-facing content. The goal is not to make every description longer. It is to make every listing useful, accurate, distinct, and complete.

Titles

Check for clarity, consistency, unnecessary keyword stuffing, duplicate titles, and missing distinguishing details.

Descriptions

Look for thin copy, boilerplate language, repeated sentences, unsupported claims, and important information buried in prose.

Product details

Confirm materials, dimensions, care, compatibility, specifications, and variant details match the actual product.

Brand Voice

Identify copy that sounds like several different companies wrote the same catalog. Decide what should be standardized before rewriting at scale.

For an individual listing, How to Refresh an Existing Product Without Starting Over provides a more focused product-level audit.

3. Review images, alt text, and visual consistency.

Images are both product information and interface. A catalog can have technically correct product data and still feel broken if the imagery is inconsistent, outdated, blurry, or incomplete.

Check whether each product has the angles shoppers need, whether imagery follows a consistent visual system, and whether obsolete images should be removed. Then review accessibility separately from filename hygiene.

For a deeper image process, see How to Build an Image Accessibility QA Standard for Your Catalog and Product Image Metadata: Filenames vs. Alt Text vs. Visible Copy.

4. Review SEO after the product itself is trustworthy.

SEO should describe a useful, accurate page. It cannot rescue a product page whose content, taxonomy, or merchandising context is wrong.

Once the product information is solid, review search-facing signals for duplication, missing context, and opportunities to better match the way customers search.

SEO elementAudit for
Page titleUnique, descriptive wording that reflects the actual product
Meta descriptionUseful summary rather than duplicate boilerplate
URLUnnecessary changes, legacy slugs, or redirects that need preservation
Internal linksConnections from relevant collections and supporting content
Search intentWhether the page answers a product-specific need or should point elsewhere

Do not change URLs casually during a cleanup. A prettier slug is rarely worth breaking an existing path without a redirect plan.

5. Audit collections as shopper destinations.

Inherited catalogs often accumulate collections because somebody needed a temporary landing page, campaign grouping, or internal merchandising shortcut. Some should stay. Some should be filters. Some should disappear.

Ask whether each collection still has a clear shopper purpose, enough relevant products, useful navigation context, and distinct intent from neighboring collections.

For the collection-specific decision framework, see How Collections Can Become Search Landing Pages.

6. Finish with merchandising and discovery.

Merchandising is where the cleaned-up catalog finally becomes useful to a shopper. Review featured products, sort order, cross-sells, inventory-aware placement, collection membership, and whether the store is making the best products easy to find.

This is deliberately last. There is little value in polishing collection order while the products inside the collection are still misclassified.

7. Prioritize the fixes by risk and reach.

A catalog audit can generate hundreds of findings. Treating them as one giant cleanup list is a good way to finish none of them.

Fix first

Incorrect product data, broken variants, misleading claims, major navigation errors, accessibility blockers, and anything affecting many products.

Fix next

Duplicate content, inconsistent taxonomy, missing structured fields, weak images, collection overlap, and SEO duplication.

Improve later

Minor copy polish, optional enrichment, edge-case merchandising, and lower-impact formatting inconsistencies.

Leave alone

Differences that are intentional, harmless, or costly to change without a meaningful shopper or operational benefit.

8. Document the catalog you are leaving behind.

An audit is not complete if the next person has to rediscover all of your decisions.

Document product-type rules, tag conventions, structured fields, vendor standards, collection logic, naming decisions, approval boundaries, and known exceptions. If a rule exists only in somebody’s memory, it is waiting to become inconsistent again.

The takeaway

Do not audit an inherited Shopify catalog in page order. Audit it in dependency order. Structure first. Then content, images, SEO, collections, and merchandising. The cleaner the foundation, the less work you have to redo later.
